# **An Agentic LLM–Time Series Framework for Predicting Drug-Induced Acute Kidney Injury: A Privacy-Preserving Approach Using Synthetic EHR Data**

## **Background**

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a severe, life-threatening clinical syndrome characterized by a rapid, precipitous decline in renal excretory function. This physiological deterioration manifests as an accumulation of metabolic waste products, fluid overload, and profound acid-base imbalances \[1\]. In the intensive care unit (ICU) setting, the prevalence of AKI is alarmingly high, affecting up to 50% of critically ill patients \[1\]. The clinical consequences of this syndrome are devastating; AKI in the ICU is associated with a mortality rate approaching 26.9%, which is nearly four times higher than the mortality observed in critically ill patients without renal impairment \[1, 2\]. Furthermore, AKI significantly extends the duration of hospitalization and drives up associated healthcare expenditures, accounting for 15% to 25% of total hospital costs in severe cases that require continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) \[1\].  
Clinically, the diagnosis and staging of AKI are standardized by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria \[3\]. The KDIGO framework depends primarily on static threshold elevations in serum creatinine—such as an absolute increase of 26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) within 48 hours—as well as sustained reductions in urine output \[3\]. However, these conventional diagnostic biomarkers are fundamentally flawed as early warning signals; they are lagging indicators \[4\]. Substantial and often irreversible structural renal damage, such as acute tubular necrosis, typically precedes measurable aberrations in serum creatinine by several hours or even days \[4\].  
Among the myriad etiologies of renal impairment, drug-induced acute kidney injury (DI-AKI) constitutes a critical, highly specific, and largely preventable subset. Nephrotoxic pharmaceutical agents account for 19% to 26% of all hospital-acquired AKI cases \[2, 5\]. DI-AKI arises from exposure to high-risk medications, including broad-spectrum antibiotics, antineoplastic agents, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and intravenous contrast media \[2, 5\]. The pathophysiology of DI-AKI is exceptionally heterogeneous. The mechanisms of injury span direct tubular toxicity, allergic acute interstitial nephritis, hemodynamically mediated reductions in glomerular filtration, and crystal nephropathy \[5\]. Because DI-AKI varies drastically by pharmacological class, individual patient phenotype, and cumulative exposure duration, generalized predictive models often fail to capture the nuanced, drug-specific temporal trajectories required to identify impending toxicity before clinical manifestation \[5, 6\].  
To overcome the diagnostic latency inherent in biomarker surveillance, the clinical informatics community has increasingly turned to predictive algorithms utilizing high-dimensional, longitudinal electronic health record (EHR) data. Initially, statistical models relied on logistic regression and static baseline variables. However, the paradigm has shifted toward deep learning architectures, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees (GBDTs) \[6, 7\]. These advanced models can process variable-length time-series data to capture the dynamic physiological decline of patients, tracking subtle permutations in vital signs and laboratory values \[6\]. While these algorithms demonstrate exceptional discriminative capacity and extended lead times, they suffer from fundamental limitations: a lack of clinical interpretability, poor generalizability across disparate healthcare systems, and an inability to dynamically synthesize unstructured clinical narratives or invoke specific medical guidelines \[8, 9, 10\].  
The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) offers a transformative solution to the limitations of traditional deep learning \[11, 12\]. Rather than functioning solely as static predictive engines, advanced LLMs can be deployed as autonomous, tool-using "agents." In an agentic framework, an LLM orchestrates complex clinical reasoning by retrieving external medical knowledge, executing code to query databases, interpreting structured tabular data, and dynamically routing patient cases to specialized, drug-specific predictive models \[11, 12\]. These clinical agents mirror the cognitive processes of multidisciplinary team consultations, bridging the gap between raw algorithmic output and auditable, protocol-constrained clinical decision support \[12\].  
Despite this technological promise, the development, training, and external validation of these multi-modal, agentic systems are severely hampered by data privacy constraints. EHRs contain highly sensitive protected health information governed by strict regulatory frameworks. To circumvent these bottlenecks, researchers have pioneered privacy-preserving synthetic data generation utilizing Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and diffusion models \[13, 14\]. These generative architectures mathematically guarantee differential privacy while maintaining the complex longitudinal and multivariate distributions of real-world patient trajectories \[13, 14\]. Consequently, the convergence of robust time-series forecasting, agentic LLM orchestration, and synthetic EHR infrastructure presents an unprecedented opportunity to accurately predict, interpret, and prevent DI-AKI.

## **Current Evidence & Numerical Findings**

The literature surrounding the prediction of acute kidney injury currently exists across four distinct but complementary domains: longitudinal time-series forecasting, drug-specific nephrotoxicity modeling, agentic LLM orchestration, and privacy-preserving synthetic data generation.

### **High-Dimensional Time-Series Forecasting for General Acute Kidney Injury**

The foundation for dynamic renal forecasting was established by deep learning models trained on massive retrospective EHR datasets. A landmark continuous prediction framework developed by Tomašev et al. analyzed a cohort of 703,782 adult patients across 172 inpatient sites and 1,062 outpatient facilities \[4\]. Demonstrating the profound efficacy of RNN architectures, the model successfully predicted 55.8% of all inpatient AKI episodes and 90.2% of severe AKI cases requiring subsequent dialysis \[4\]. Crucially, the system provided an actionable lead time of up to 48 hours before clinical diagnosis, maintaining a clinically viable ratio of 2 false alerts for every true alert (representing a specificity of 82.7% against a baseline prevalence of 13.4%) \[4\].  
Subsequent studies optimized these architectures for the intensive care unit, where data density is vastly superior but patient physiological volatility is higher. Tan et al. engineered a multimodal framework leveraging the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, integrating LSTM embeddings for structured time-series measurements with BioMedBERT representations for unstructured clinical notes \[7\]. This multimodal approach achieved an Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) of 0.888 for general AKI prediction and an exceptional 0.997 for continuous renal replacement therapy forecasting, boasting a lead time of at least 12 hours ahead of clinical manifestation \[7\]. Precision-recall metrics similarly demonstrated high fidelity, yielding an Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) of 0.727 for AKI and 0.840 for dialysis requirement \[7\].  
External validation studies emphasize that performance heavily depends on rigorous algorithmic alignment with KDIGO labeling. Lyu et al. demonstrated that integrating the complete KDIGO criteria—specifically factoring in temporal urine output alongside serum creatinine—materially improved prediction over creatinine-only models \[3\]. Utilizing GBDTs with time-stacking techniques, their model achieved an AUPRC of 65.7%, substantially outperforming a baseline LSTM architecture which achieved 62.6% \[3\]. Additionally, Alfieri et al. validated a continuous machine learning model predicting KDIGO stage 2 and 3 AKI across 16,760 ICU patients in three countries \[8\]. The model achieved an internal AUROC of 0.884 and maintained robust discrimination externally (AUROC ranging from 0.877 to 0.911), consistently predicting severe injury at least 14 hours in advance of physiological decompensation \[8\]. Supporting this geographic transportability, Hourani et al. deployed an interpretable XGBoost model across 51,833 ICU stays, achieving an internal AUC of 0.88 with a 24-hour lead time, while maintaining a temporal validation AUC of 0.84 (sensitivity 0.76, specificity 0.79) during the COVID-19 pandemic and an external geographic validation AUC of 0.82 (sensitivity 0.73, specificity 0.86) \[9\].

### **The Complexities and Predictive Architectures of Drug-Induced Nephrotoxicity**

While general AKI predictors are highly sensitive, DI-AKI presents a distinct challenge due to phenotypic and mechanistic heterogeneity. Yousif and Awdishu highlighted that temporal associations alone frequently misclassify AKI etiology; accurate DI-AKI modeling requires expert adjudication and the incorporation of pharmacokinetic parameters such as cumulative drug dose, duration of exposure, and therapeutic concentration \[11\]. In an evaluation of the Drug-Induced Renal Injury Consortium (DIRECT) study, which included 314 rigorously adjudicated AKI cases (of which 86% were explicitly drug-induced), an L1-regularized multivariable logistic regression model achieved an AUROC of 0.86 \[12\]. The model identified variables such as acute serum creatinine trends, baseline vascular capacity, pyuria, and concurrent contrast media exposure as primary predictors of toxicity \[12\].  
Efforts to operationalize DI-AKI prediction in clinical practice include the adult translation of the pediatric Nephrotoxic Injury Negated by Just-in-time Action (NINJA) program. Griffin et al. evaluated 14,480 hospitalized adults representing 37,300 exposure events to high-risk nephrotoxic regimens (defined precisely as the concurrent administration of ![][image1] 3 nephrotoxins or intravenous aminoglycosides/vancomycin for ![][image1] 3 consecutive days) \[13\]. Utilizing a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) based RNN incorporating 85 clinical variables, the model predicted AKI within 48 hours with a precision of 0.60 \[13\]. Most importantly, the algorithm mitigated the pervasive issue of alert fatigue, decreasing false alerts from 2.5 down to 0.7 per true AKI case \[13\]. The artificial neural network identified the lowest hemoglobin, lowest blood pressure, and highest white blood cell count as top physiological predictors, alongside specific pharmacological exposures such as acyclovir, piperacillin-tazobactam, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) \[13\].  
Because nephrotoxicity profiles vary drastically by pharmacological agent, several recent studies have generated highly specialized, drug-specific predictors. In a cohort of 1,392 patients with multidrug-resistant Gram-negative infections, Chiu et al. applied categorical boosting with oversampling to predict colistin-induced nephrotoxicity \[14\]. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.823, an AUPRC of 0.737, and a sensitivity of 0.860, identifying a critical toxicity threshold at a cumulative dosage of 4.0 mg/kg body weight/day \[14\]. For vancomycin—a ubiquitous glycopeptide antibiotic with well-documented renal clearance limitations—Aghamirzaei et al. deployed a stacking ensemble machine learning algorithm for 314 ICU patients \[15\]. The model achieved an exceptional AUROC of 0.94, predicting AKI onset with 92% accuracy, 93% precision, and 92% sensitivity, relying heavily on glucose variability, patient age, and baseline creatinine \[15\]. Concurrently, Heo et al. evaluated vancomycin toxicity across a 6-hospital distributed research network comprising 39,655 total patients using an Interpretable Multivariable LSTM (IMV-LSTM) \[16\]. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.92 for vancomycin, demonstrating a median time-to-onset of 12 days (interquartile range: 5 to 25 days), while achieving an AUROC of 0.94 for acyclovir with a delayed median onset of 23 days (interquartile range: 10 to 41 days) \[16\]. Further confirming the validity of predicting vancomycin-associated creatinine elevations, Fan et al. analyzed 10,288 ICU adults using a CatBoost model, achieving an AUROC of 0.818 (sensitivity 0.800, specificity 0.681) and a high negative predictive value of 0.900, identifying hyperphosphatemia as the strongest physiological driver of impending injury \[17\]. Finally, in the domain of oncology, Ambe et al. tracked 1,253 patients receiving cisplatin chemotherapy \[18\]. In this cohort, 9.5% developed clinical AKI with a median onset of 7 days \[18\]. Patients developing AKI received a higher mean initial dose (77.9 ± 27.1 mg/m$^2$) compared to non-AKI patients (69.3 ± 22.6 mg/m$^2$) \[18\]. Their CatBoost classification model yielded a ROC-AUC of 0.78, utilizing SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify concurrent intravenous magnesium administration as a protective variable against tubular necrosis \[18\].

### **Agentic Large Language Models in Clinical Decision Support**

While quantitative deep learning predictors accurately forecast risk, they are functionally rigid; they fail to autonomously synthesize missing variables, query medical literature mid-inference, or provide human-readable clinical rationale that justifies their statistical outputs \[10, 19\]. Agentic LLM frameworks—systems wherein a language model iteratively plans, executes external tools, and interprets the results—bridge this exact methodological gap.  
Goodell et al. demonstrated that unaugmented LLMs fail frequently at clinical calculations, outputting incorrect responses in 33% of trials (n \= 212\) due to arithmetic hallucinations \[19\]. However, when augmented with code interpreters and task-specific tools, error rates dropped dramatically; LLaMa and GPT-based models showed a 5.5-fold and 13-fold reduction in incorrect responses, respectively \[19\]. Expanding on this tool-use paradigm, Jin et al. introduced AgentMD, an autonomous language agent capable of curating and executing validated clinical calculators \[20\]. AgentMD autonomously aggregated a diverse toolkit of 2,164 executable calculators from the biomedical literature, passing strict unit tests with over a 90% success rate \[20\]. When evaluated on the rigorous RiskQA benchmark, the agentic application achieved an accuracy of 87.7%, significantly outperforming standard chain-of-thought prompting using GPT-4, which achieved a mere 40.9% accuracy \[20\]. Similarly, Liu et al. developed RiskAgent, a copilot designed to collaborate with hundreds of clinical decision tools across 387 distinct disease risk scenarios \[21\]. Evaluated across 12,352 complex clinical questions spanning 154 diseases, the 8-billion parameter RiskAgent achieved an accuracy of 76.33%, vastly outperforming baseline commercial models such as GPT-4o, which scored 38.39% \[21\].  
In the context of longitudinal EHR tabular data, Shi et al. introduced EHRAgent, an LLM empowered with coding capabilities to interact directly with structured databases \[22\]. By iteratively generating Python or SQL code, executing database queries, and learning from execution feedback and error messages, EHRAgent improved the success rate of complex clinical reasoning tasks by up to 29.6% over the strongest non-agentic baselines \[22\].  
Specific to nephrology, pioneering multi-agent frameworks are beginning to emerge. The ColaCare architecture operates on a Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) philosophy, utilizing distinct "DoctorAgents" and a "MetaAgent" to analyze patient data \[23\]. ColaCare demonstrated superior performance in mortality and readmission prediction by fusing numerical expert model outputs with LLM-driven reasoning reports, engaging in evidence-based debate before finalizing predictions \[23\]. Furthermore, Gordon et al. introduced AKIBoards, a structure-following multi-agent system utilizing an explicit global model as a prior belief matrix to prevent diagnostic drift \[24\]. This architecture achieved an Average Precision (AP) of 0.195 in predicting AKI 48 hours before onset, substantially outperforming non-structure-following baselines (AP 0.141) \[24\]. Finally, Shi et al. designed AKI-Detector, an ICU-focused multi-agent system that combines structured EHR machine learning, LLMs, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \[25\]. By grounding LLM reasoning directly in algorithmic predictions to prevent hallucinations, AKI-Detector achieved an accuracy of 0.827, a precision of 0.672, a recall of 0.542, and an F1-score of 0.600 on the MIMIC-IV cohort, providing balanced statistical performance alongside highly interpretable, text-based clinical reports \[25\].

### **Privacy-Preserving Synthetic Electronic Health Records**

The deployment of these highly complex agentic and time-series architectures necessitates vast quantities of training data, inherently violating the data localization protocols of most hospital networks due to privacy risks. Synthetic EHR generation offers a mathematically rigorous solution. Yoon et al. developed EHR-Safe, a generative modeling framework utilizing sequential encoder-decoder networks coupled with generative adversarial networks (GANs) to process heterogeneous, sparse, and time-varying features \[26\]. Evaluated across multiple privacy and fidelity metrics, predictive models trained exclusively on EHR-Safe synthetic data demonstrated less than a 3% difference in accuracy when compared to models trained on the original real-world datasets, successfully mitigating membership inference and re-identification risks \[26\].  
Wang et al. addressed the specific challenge of irregular and heterogeneous time-series sampling with IGAMT (Imitative Generative Adversarial Mixed-embedding Transformer) \[27\]. IGAMT successfully processed complex combinations of temporal features, structurally missing values, and discrete static variables while enforcing strict differential privacy (DP) budgets \[27\]. By utilizing a dual adversarial structure combined with an imitator network to control DP noise, IGAMT ultimately outperformed traditional autoencoder baselines in both visual data resemblance and downstream clinical utility \[27\].  
Crucially, synthetic data integration directly enhances DI-AKI predictive modeling by resolving critical class imbalances. Ramazani et al. evaluated the nephrotoxic risks of concurrent vancomycin and ceftazidime/avibactam (VAN-AVI) therapy in a retrospective cohort \[28\]. In their dataset, only 4.3% of patients (4 out of 92\) receiving the combined therapy experienced new-onset AKI, creating extreme class imbalance that prohibited standard algorithmic training \[28\]. By employing Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) alongside Conditional Tabular GANs (CTGAN) and Tabular Variational Autoencoders (TVAE) to generate realistic synthetic trajectories, the researchers identified a substantial risk increase for the VAN-AVI group, recording a Hazard Ratio (HR) of 3.47 (95% CI: 1.97 to 6.11) compared to vancomycin alone \[28\]. Machine learning models augmented with this synthetic data dramatically outperformed those utilizing original data alone, increasing the F1-score for 30-day AKI prediction to a peak of 0.80 utilizing an augmented XGBoost classifier \[28\].

## **Justification for the Present Review**

Despite rapid, isolated advancements across clinical informatics, significant research gaps persist at the intersection of these disparate domains. Robust longitudinal AKI forecasting models currently exist, yet they remain largely disassociated from the causal, drug-specific etiologies required for targeted clinical intervention. Concurrently, while tool-using clinical LLM agents demonstrate remarkable capabilities in executing static medical calculators and extracting tabular data, they have yet to be systematically integrated as dynamic orchestration layers capable of routing complex, evolving patient trajectories to external, highly specific nephrotoxicity models. Furthermore, because training such an integrated, multi-model agentic system relies on massive and diverse data exposures that violate institutional privacy boundaries, differential privacy through synthetic EHR generation is a mandatory—yet underexamined—infrastructural requirement. This review synthesizes these previously fragmented lines of evidence to justify a unified architectural paradigm: an agentic framework that leverages synthetic longitudinal data to train, and LLMs to orchestrate, drug-specific AKI prediction.

## **Aim of the Review**

The primary aim of this review is to comprehensively evaluate and synthesize the current state of the literature regarding longitudinal acute kidney injury prediction, drug-induced nephrotoxicity, agentic large language models, and privacy-preserving synthetic data generation. By critically appraising the numerical efficacy, algorithmic capabilities, and limitations of existing models, this manuscript seeks to define a clear, evidence-based roadmap for the development of an integrated, auditable, and dynamically routed Agentic LLM–Time Series framework designed to predict and prevent drug-induced acute kidney injury while strictly adhering to data privacy protocols.
